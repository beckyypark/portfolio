Rebecca Park

Bethany House - Summer Service Learning Program

August 21, 2022

April 2022 - August 2022

Background#

During my freshman year summer, I decided to take part in Notre Dame’s Summer Service Learning Program (SSLP). The SSLP Program is an eight-week, service-learning course in which students are placed at more than 170 nonprofit agencies across the country. They work at least 35 hours per week and many times live in community with the people they serve.

For my SSLP I wanted to give back to the community I was raised and born in, Rochester, NY. Rochester is a diverse city and is home to many migrants. Being the birth place of many manufacturing industries: Kodak, Bausch and Lomb, and Xerox, many migrants moved to Rochester, in hopes of achieving the American Dream - the enduring belief that through hard work, determination, and initiative, any individual can achieve upward mobility, prosperity, and a better life regardless of their background. This was same dream that brought my immigrant parents to Rochester.

Growing up in the suburbs I felt I was naive to the reality in which many have to endure and I’ve always wanted to fill in this gap. Thus, for my SSLP I was placed at Bethany House, a Catholic Worker house that provides temporary housing, assistance and support to women and children in Rochester, NY afflicted with homelessness and instability from domestic violence, incarceration, addiction, mental health, physical illness, or poverty since 1977. Their mission is beautifully captured in their motto: “We will love you, until you can love yourself.”

My Experience#

At Bethany House, I took on both operational and relational responsibilities. I supervised food pantry operations, prepared supply bags, logged client information, and scheduled appointments. Within the home itself, I was personally responsible for the well-being of resident women and children, monitoring shared spaces, supporting accountability systems, and ensuring the house functioned smoothly. But the most formative parts of my experience were not logistical, they were relational.

I witnessed the deep emotional toll that unstable housing and limited institutional support can have on families. One moment that has stayed with me involved a mother and daughter preparing to leave the house after their 45-day stay, still without permanent housing secured. The mother described feeling unsupported by her caseworker, saying, “I feel like I’m choking… like I can’t breathe.”

In that moment, I felt the tension between individual effort and systemic constraint. I saw firsthand how missed appointments, communication breakdowns, and burnout among social workers contributed to mistrust between clients and case managers. What might appear as “noncompliance” on paper was often masked by mental health challenges, scheduling barriers, or simple exhaustion from navigating poverty. Through this experience, I began to analyze the broader system: Case managers often carry overwhelming caseloads. Compensation and mental health support for social workers are limited relative to the emotional labor required. Clients frequently feel unheard or are prematurely categorized.

Rather than seeing fault on one side, I came to understand the issue as structural. A resource and systems design problem affecting both helpers and those seeking help.

Takeaways#

My time at Bethany House reshaped how I understand justice, policy, and community. I do not believe that clients or case managers are inherently adversarial. Instead, I believe that insufficient resources, limited training, and structural inefficiencies create unnecessary friction between individuals who ultimately share the same goal: stability and well-being.

The principle of solidarity — the idea that we are responsible for one another — became tangible to me. Justice is not abstract. It is found in returned phone calls, manageable caseloads, livable wages, trauma-informed care, and systems designed for human dignity rather than administrative efficiency alone.

Rochester once gave my immigrant family a foothold through community support and shelters. That legacy of compassion shaped my life. My experience at Bethany House deepened my commitment to ensuring that social systems function not merely as bureaucracies, but as instruments of genuine care.

Going forward, I am committed to applying both analytical rigor and human-centered thinking to social systems, identifying inefficiencies, reallocating resources strategically, and designing structures that serve the whole person.

What I would do differently#

Through my reflection, there are several things I would approach differently:

  1. Engage Case Managers Directly While I observed client frustrations, I would now seek structured conversations with case managers themselves to better understand their constraints.

  2. Collect Quantitative Data On-Site Although I incorporated national statistics into my proposal, I would gather more site-specific data like average response times, caseload sizes, and appointment attendance rates.

  3. Incorporate Technology Solutions I would explore whether automated appointment reminders, shared digital scheduling platforms, or centralized communication systems could reduce friction caused by missed calls and scheduling confusion.
